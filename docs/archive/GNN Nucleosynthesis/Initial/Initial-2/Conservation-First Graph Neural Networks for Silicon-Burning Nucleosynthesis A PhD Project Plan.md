# Graph Neural Networks for Stellar Nucleosynthesis Reaction Networks: A PhD Project-Design and Planning Report

## TL;DR
- **Build a stoichiometry-respecting, flux-form GNN that emulates MESA's nuclear burning in the Si-burning regime, and benchmark it offline against the published Grichener et al. 2025 NNN MLP baseline using their public Zenodo data (record 14873443) — this is your fast first paper, achievable in a few months with zero new training-data generation.** Your decisive differentiators from the competing NuGNN (arXiv:2606.04491) are: (i) a *different* astrophysical regime (Si-burning/CCSN progenitors vs. Type I X-ray bursts), (ii) *exact* baryon/charge conservation by construction (NuGNN only learns Σ ΔX=0 softly), and (iii) a head-to-head comparison against a *published* MLP baseline on identical public data.
- **The single riskiest step is Phase 3: in-the-loop MESA coupling over ~10³–10⁴ timesteps to core collapse.** Error accumulation and solver feedback can destabilize the structure solve; this is also where the NuGNN group is most likely to scoop you. Mitigate with the Grichener-collaboration moat (bbq pipeline, MESA expertise, split-burning operator) and a guaranteed fallback-to-solver gate.
- **Front-load conservation and the offline benchmark.** Hard conservation is cheap to implement (a fixed stoichiometric-matrix projection), is the most defensible scientific claim, and is exactly the property NuGNN lacks. Generating new bbq data costs ~few×10⁵ CPU-hr per regime per Grichener et al. — a budget you do *not* have on a 24-core machine, so design the whole first year to live on existing public data.

## Key Findings

**1. The benchmark is solid and the numbers check out.** Grichener et al. 2025 ("Nuclear Neural Networks," arXiv:2503.00115; ApJS 279, 49; DOI 10.3847/1538-4365/ade717; authors Aldana Grichener, Mathieu Renzo, Wolfgang E. Kerzendorf, Rob Farmer, Selma E. de Mink, Earl Patrick Bellinger, Chi-kwan Chan, Nutan Chen, Ebraheem Farag, Stephen Justham) build a fully-connected MLP ("NNN") emulator of MESA r23.05.1's nuclear burning via the bbq wrapper (Farmer 2023, DOI 10.5281/zenodo.7585202), for the softwired networks mesa_80 and mesa_151. Verified load-bearing numbers: training data sampled with a **Sobol quasi-random sampler** over **10^9.2 K < T < 10^9.9 K and 10^7 < ρ < 10^9 g/cm³** (0.45 < Ye < 0.5); separate models per logarithmically-spaced timestep; Ye improvement of **280–660%**, Abar/Zbar **150–360%**, nuclear energy generation **250–750%** over the approx21 small net. For neutrino losses, the paper states verbatim that "the NNNs perform 100−10⁶% better than the small net for short timesteps, while resulting in comparable and larger errors for dt ≳ 0.1 s" — i.e. the dramatic neutrino-loss improvement is real only at short timesteps and *degrades* at long Δt, a nuance to preserve. The paper's headline speedup is given in a matched context: "NNNs have the potential of accelerating stellar evolution simulations performed with large nuclear reaction networks by a factor of **20–34** (Fig. 7)." They chose **12 hidden layers for mesa_80 and 9 for mesa_151**. The public Zenodo record 14873443 contains MESA stellar models, training/test sets, trained NNN models, and analysis scripts; the *complete* bbq runs (extra timesteps, extended density) total **4 TB and are available only on request**. bbq uses the Bulirsch–Stoer algorithm at constant T, ρ. Limitations they state explicitly: large per-isotope errors, no hard baryon/charge conservation, error accumulation untested over rollouts, no extrapolation, MESA coupling not yet implemented.

**2. The competitor (NuGNN) is real, strong, but leaves your niche open.** Kim, Chae, Ko, Mumpower & Smith (arXiv:2606.04491, submitted 3 June 2026) built the first GNN surrogate for a nuclear reaction network. Per the abstract, they "develop a deep learning surrogate solver for a large **690-isotope** nuclear reaction network under general **Type I X-ray burst** conditions using a graph neural network, NuGNN… NuGNN directly reflects the structure of the reaction network through heterogeneous isotope and reaction nodes and message-passing along reaction connections." Architecture: isotope nodes and reaction nodes, three connection types (isotope→reaction, reaction→isotope, isotope→isotope), **five message-passing blocks**, graph attention, and a two-channel sigmoid output head. Affiliations (correcting a common assumption): C.H. Kim, K.Y. Chae & S. Ko at **Sungkyunkwan University** (Suwon); M.R. Mumpower at **Obsidian Research (Fort Wayne, IN) and the University of Notre Dame** — *not* LANL; M.S. Smith at **Stellar Science Solutions** (Lenoir City, TN). It targets T ∈ [10⁶, 2.5×10⁹] K, ρ ∈ [10⁻², 10⁸] g/cm³, Δt ∈ [10⁻¹⁵, 10⁻⁴] s. Critically for your differentiation:
   - **NuGNN predicts ΔX directly and does NOT enforce conservation by construction.** It learns Σ ΔXᵢ = 0 only implicitly from data, backed by a retry-with-smaller-Δt heuristic. The paper states they *tried* a conservation loss term / output activation and abandoned it because their log-target preprocessing made it unstable. **This is your opening: hard conservation in the Si-burning regime is unclaimed.**
   - Accuracy: total mean-absolute-error in signed-log space of 0.037 (NuGNN) vs. 0.11 (Res-U-Net) vs. 0.56 (FNN); for physically realistic ("Type A") data this is ≈1.8% (all X) / 8.4% (non-zero X) in linear ΔX. Per the abstract, NuGNN "consistently achieves significantly better accuracy with errors of only a few percent… when implemented in the network evolution code in place of the original solver, NuGNN successfully reproduces the final abundance patterns, whereas the other architectures fail to do so" — Table 2 gives a 3–10× accuracy edge over baselines, with in-solver deviations from the true solver mostly below ~15%.
   - Speed/hardware: trained on **90,000 samples** (72k/9k/9k split); inference 19.3 ms (PyTorch) → **1.9 ms with TensorRT on an RTX 5090**, vs. PRISM solver on an Intel Xeon Platinum 8360Y CPU. **This is a hardware-asymmetric (GPU-vs-CPU) comparison with no clean speedup factor stated** — a methodological pitfall you must avoid. Note NuGNN is actually the *slowest* of its three NN surrogates (1.9 ms vs. FNN's 0.1 ms); its advantage is accuracy, not raw speed.

**3. Hard conservation by construction is a mature idea in adjacent fields and directly portable.** Döppel & Votsmeier's atom-conserving CRNN (building on Ji & Deng 2021, arXiv:2002.09062; Combustion & Flame 2024) enforces atom conservation by embedding a conservation matrix (the reduced column echelon form of the null-space basis of the molecular matrix) as a fixed linear layer, which "improves training stability and speed, offers robustness against noisy and missing data, and requires less data overall." The flux-form principle — predict reaction fluxes, then map to abundance changes via the (fixed, known) stoichiometric matrix — guarantees conservation to machine precision because the stoichiometric matrix has baryon number and charge in its null space by construction. Dynami-CAL GraphNet (Sharma & Fink, Nat. Commun. 17:1045, Jan 2026; arXiv:2501.07373) demonstrates the GNN analogue: enforcing pairwise conservation (linear/angular momentum) via edge-local frames yields "stable error accumulation over extended rollouts, effective extrapolations to unseen configurations." MACE (Maes et al. 2024, ApJ 969:79; arXiv:2405.03274) reports that enforcing element conservation as a *soft* loss "slowed down the training immensely, because… the jacobian of the decoder neural network needs to be calculated" — corroborating that *architectural* (hard) conservation is the better route than a penalty term.

**4. The stiffness/rollout problem is well-charted.** GNS (Sanchez-Gonzalez et al. 2020, arXiv:2002.09405) established that "the main determinants of long-term performance were the number of message-passing steps, and mitigating the accumulation of error by corrupting the training data with noise" (random-walk noise so the model sees its own error distribution). MeshGraphNets (Pfaff et al. 2020, arXiv:2010.03409) extended this to mesh dynamics with adaptive resolution/size transfer. These are directly applicable: naive autoregressive GNN rollouts diverge because one-step models never see off-trajectory states. Van de Bor et al. (2025) found Branca & Pallottini's (2024) DeepONet ISM emulator "prone to instability due to the accumulation of prediction errors" under iteration for exactly this reason; the fixes are noise injection (Holdship 2021) or training the iterative update directly (Maes 2024).

**5. The CCSN-progenitor science case is genuine.** Farmer, Fields, Petermann et al. 2016 (arXiv:1611.01207, ApJS 227:22) showed, verbatim, that "at the onset of core collapse we find ≈30% variations in the central electron fraction and mass locations of the main nuclear burning shells, a minimum of ≈127 isotopes is needed to attain convergence of these values at the ≈10% level" — which is why mesa_80 is insufficient and mesa_151+ matters. MESA's operator-split burning (op_split_burn; Jermyn et al. 2023, ApJS 265:15) is the exact hook for in-the-loop emulator coupling, since it already decouples burning from structure at high T (T ≳ 3×10⁹ K) using a semi-implicit midpoint rule with adaptive sub-steps.

## Details

### Section 1 — Technical Formulation

**1(a) Inductive biases of the isotope-node/reaction-edge graph.**

The natural graph is **heterogeneous and bipartite**: isotope nodes and reaction nodes, with reactant→reaction and reaction→product edges (NuGNN's exact structure). Because many nuclear reactions involve ≥2 reactants/products (e.g., the triple-α, (α,p) chains, ³He+⁴He), reactions are most faithfully **hyperedges**; the bipartite reaction-node construction is the standard, computationally tractable way to represent a hypergraph (cf. ChemHGNN, arXiv:2506.11041, and Rxn-Hypergraph, arXiv:2201.01196, both for chemistry, which "naturally model multi-reactant reactions through hyperedges"). I recommend the bipartite reaction-node formulation over explicit hypergraph-convolution layers: it is what NuGNN validated for nuclear networks, and pynucastro exports exactly this connectivity (it can emit a NetworkX graph).

*Conservation — the central design decision.* Baryon number A and charge Z are linear functions of the abundance vector. The nuclear ODE is Ẏᵢ = Σ (stoichiometric coefficients) × (fluxes). If the network **predicts per-reaction fluxes Fⱼ ≥ 0 and computes ΔYᵢ = Σⱼ νᵢⱼ Fⱼ Δt with the fixed stoichiometric matrix ν**, then because every nuclear reaction conserves A and Z (Σᵢ Aᵢ νᵢⱼ = 0 and Σᵢ Zᵢ νᵢⱼ = 0 for every reaction j), the predicted ΔY conserves baryon number and charge **to machine precision regardless of how wrong the fluxes are**. This is the decisive advantage over both NNN (no conservation) and NuGNN (soft/learned conservation only). Σ Xᵢ = 1 follows from baryon conservation since Xᵢ = Aᵢ Yᵢ. This is the direct nuclear analogue of the atom-conserving CRNN's conservation-matrix layer and of Dynami-CAL's edge-level momentum conservation. Soft penalties (an extra loss term ‖Σ ΔXᵢ‖) should be *avoided as the primary mechanism*: MACE found them slow, and NuGNN found them unstable with log-targets.

A subtlety to flag as a **design caveat**: weak reactions (electron captures, β-decays) change Z at fixed A and change Ye — these are precisely the reactions that matter for the CCSN Ye, and they are tabulated (T, ρYe)-dependent rates (pynucastro's TabularRate, from Suzuki et al. 2016). The stoichiometric matrix must include lepton bookkeeping so flux-form updates reproduce the correct Ye drift; baryon number is still conserved exactly, but Ye is *supposed* to change, so your conservation diagnostic must distinguish "exactly conserved A" from "physically evolving Ye."

*Size transfer (mesa_80 → mesa_151 → mesa_204).* The GNS/MeshGraphNets literature is encouraging: GNS generalizes from thousands of particles in training to "at least an order of magnitude more particles at test time," and MeshGraphNets adapts mesh resolution at inference — because message passing is local and parameters are shared across nodes/edges. A GNN whose parameters live on *node-type* and *reaction-type* embeddings (not on a fixed isotope index) can in principle be evaluated on a larger isotope set without retraining. **However, this is a genuine research question, not a guarantee:** the new isotopes in mesa_151 \ mesa_80 are neutron-rich species dynamically important precisely where mesa_80 fails, so the *physics* in the transfer region is genuinely new. My assessment (labeled as a reasoned estimate): expect zero-shot size transfer to capture qualitative behavior but require fine-tuning on a modest mesa_151 set to hit per-isotope accuracy. This is a Phase-2 deliverable, not a freebie.

**1(b) Candidate architectures and stiffness remedies.**

Ranked recommendation:
1. **Flux-form bipartite GNN (primary).** Isotope+reaction nodes, ~4–6 message-passing steps (NuGNN used five), per-reaction non-negative flux head, stoichiometric-matrix projection to ΔY. Conservation by construction. This is your Paper 1 architecture.
2. **GNN encoder + latent neural ODE (Phase 2).** Encode the graph state to a latent vector, evolve with a neural ODE for variable Δt (the MACE pattern, arXiv:2405.03274), decode. Buys true continuous-time / variable-dt without training 9 separate models. Risk: conservation in latent space is not automatic — keep the flux-form decoder.
3. **GNN + DeepONet/operator head (Phase 2 alternative).** A branch/trunk operator head taking Δt as the trunk input (Goswami et al. 2023, arXiv:2302.12645, CMAME 419:116674; Branca & Pallottini 2024, A&A 684 A203, DOI 10.1051/0004-6361/202449193, achieved up to ~128× speedup but with documented iterative instability). Good for spanning the 10⁻⁶–10² s timestep range with one model.
4. **Attention/transformer-on-graph (incremental).** NuGNN already uses graph attention; useful but not a differentiator.

*Why naive autoregressive rollouts diverge, and the remedies:* one-step-trained models never see their own accumulated error, so at inference they are pushed off-distribution and error compounds super-linearly (the GNS diagnosis). Remedies, in priority order: (i) **training-noise injection** (GNS random-walk noise — cheapest, highest-impact); (ii) **multi-step / pushforward rollout losses** (backprop through K predicted steps); (iii) **log-space dynamics** for abundances spanning 20+ decades (NuGNN preprocesses to signed-log; Döppel & Votsmeier use an asinh transform); (iv) **stiffness-aware time parameterization** (predict on a logarithmic Δt grid, as both NNN and NuGNN do). The flux-form structure itself also damps divergence: conserved quantities cannot drift, removing one whole failure mode.

**1(c) Inputs, outputs, and which constraints go where.**

*Node features (isotopes):* Z, N, A, mass excess/binding energy, one-/two-proton separation energies (NuGNN used these), T-dependent partition functions (available in pynucastro), current Xᵢ (in log/asinh space), and learned (Z,N) embeddings. *Edge/reaction-node features:* Q-value, REACLIB rate-fit coefficients or the evaluated log-rate at current T (and ρYe for tabulated weak rates), reaction-type embedding (REACLIB chapter / (p,γ),(α,γ),(γ,α),(p,α),(α,p),β⁺,e-capture — NuGNN used a 7-type embedding). *Global features:* T, ρ, Δt.

*Output targets beyond Ye:* per-reaction fluxes (primary, internal), from which you derive ΔXᵢ, nuclear energy generation rate e_nuc, neutrino loss rate, Abar, Zbar, and Ye. Predicting fluxes rather than ΔX directly is what enables both conservation *and* a physically consistent e_nuc (computed from Σ Q-value × flux).

*Hard (in architecture):* baryon number, charge, Σ Xᵢ = 1 — all via the flux-form stoichiometric projection. *Soft (in loss):* energy consistency (q = e_nuc + neutrino terms should match Σ Qⱼ Fⱼ and the mass-excess accounting), non-negativity of fluxes (or enforce via softplus head), and optionally agreement with NSE/QSE equilibria at long Δt and high T (a physics-anchoring regularizer — the Si-burning regime is near NSE, which both constrains and helps the model).

### Section 2 — Data Strategy

**What exists publicly (use this for Year 1):** Zenodo 14873443 — mesa_80 and mesa_151 training/test sets (the Sobol grids at the 9 fixed timesteps), trained NNN models (your baseline to beat), MESA stellar models defining the parameter space, and analysis scripts. MESA r23.05.1 and bbq (Farmer 2023) are open. pynucastro (arXiv:1803.08920; ApJ 2023, DOI 10.3847/1538-4357/acbaff) provides REACLIB rates (>80,000 rates via ReacLibLibrary), tabulated weak rates (TabularLibrary, Suzuki et al. 2016), Q-values, partition functions, separation energies, and graph export for constructing your GNN's adjacency and edge features.

**What you must generate (and when):** (i) variable-Δt training data — the public sets are at 9 fixed timesteps, so a continuous-time model needs new bbq runs at intermediate Δt; (ii) trajectory/rollout data (off-trajectory states) for multi-step losses; (iii) other regimes (O-burning, lower-T/ρ for ECSNe/PPISNe); (iv) larger nets (mesa_204). The 4 TB of extra-timestep/extended-density bbq runs already exist on request from Grichener — **negotiate access through the collaboration before generating anything yourself.**

**Compute cost — explicit assumptions.** Grichener et al. quote "few×10⁵ CPU-hours per ~10⁶ samples." Taking that at face value gives **≈0.1–0.5 CPU-hr/sample** (the paper's own figure, used as an estimate). On your 24-core personal machine running continuously: 24 cores × 24 h × 30 d ≈ 17,000 core-hours/month, i.e. **~35,000–170,000 samples/month** — roughly an order of magnitude short of one fresh 10⁶-sample grid per month. *Conclusion: regenerating a Grichener-scale dataset for even one new regime is a multi-month, cluster-scale job — flag explicitly that this needs an institutional CPU allocation, not your personal hardware.* This is the strongest single argument for (a) living on public data in Year 1 and (b) securing the Grichener 4 TB and an institutional CPU allocation early.

**GPU training cost (estimate, labeled).** A NuGNN-scale GNN (five message-passing blocks, width-64, ~10⁵–10⁶ parameters; NuGNN's total parameter count was not reported) trained on ~10⁵–10⁶ samples is *small* by modern standards. On a single RTX Pro 6000 (96 GB), I estimate **hours to ~1–2 days per training run** (reasoned estimate from comparable GNN-simulator training; not a cited benchmark). Your several RTX Pro 6000s plus the RTX 5070/5070 Ti are more than adequate for the offline phases; hyperparameter sweeps, not single runs, will dominate GPU time. (NuGNN trained ~72k samples; LR 10⁻³ for 1,000 epochs then 200 epochs at 10⁻⁴ — a modest budget.)

**Avoiding Grichener's diversity failure mode.** They found that sampling compositions *only* from full stellar models gave insufficient diversity and poor generalization; their fix was broad Sobol sampling including unphysical compositions. Adopt and extend this: (i) **quasi-random (Sobol) sampling over the composition simplex** with the Ye constraint; (ii) **include off-trajectory and unphysical states** for rollout robustness (the GNS noise-injection rationale in data form); (iii) consider **active/adaptive sampling** or **DeePODE's evolutionary Monte Carlo sampling** (Yao et al. 2025, arXiv:2504.14180; applied to nuclear reacting flows in the ApJ paper DOI 10.3847/1538-4357/adf331, which "replaces conventional stiff ODE solvers with deep learning models trained through evolutionary Monte Carlo sampling… ensuring generalization across varied thermonuclear and hydrodynamic conditions"). DeePODE explicitly adapts sample density to local gradient/timescale structure and was shown to beat manifold sampling for generalization in stiff nuclear reacting flows — the principled answer to "where do I spend my expensive bbq calls."

### Section 4 — Validation Methodology

1. **Error-accumulation / rollout tests.** Reproduce Grichener's 1000-step constant-(T,ρ) rollout protocol first (apples-to-apples with NNN), then extend to varying (T,ρ) trajectories pulled from real MESA pre-SN tracks. Report per-step and cumulative error growth; this is where flux-form conservation should visibly outperform.
2. **Conservation diagnostics.** Quantify baryon-number drift, charge drift, Σ Xᵢ − 1, and energy consistency (e_nuc + neutrino losses vs. Σ Qⱼ Fⱼ from composition change). For the flux-form architecture, demonstrate baryon/charge conservation **at machine precision (≈10⁻¹⁴ relative)** — a headline, falsifiable claim that NuGNN cannot make. Show explicitly that Ye still evolves correctly (weak reactions) while A is conserved.
3. **Ground-truth comparisons.** bbq/MESA net as primary ground truth; cross-check with SkyNet and WinNet for independent solver validation. Report per-isotope error *distributions* (not just means — this was NNN's weak point), plus Ye, Abar, Zbar, e_nuc, neutrino metrics at each of the 9 timesteps.
4. **Speed — avoid the NuGNN pitfall.** Report **both** matched-hardware (GPU vs. GPU, or CPU vs. CPU) **and** deployment-realistic (your trained model on RTX Pro 6000 vs. bbq on the same node's CPU) timings, clearly labeled. Contrast with Grichener's matched-context 20–34× figure rather than NuGNN's hardware-asymmetric framing; never quote a single GPU-vs-CPU "speedup factor."
5. **Out-of-distribution detection.** Test on compositions/(T,ρ) outside the training box; report graceful degradation and an OOD flag (e.g., via predicted-flux uncertainty or an ensemble/MC-dropout estimate). This feeds the fallback-to-solver gate.
6. **"Good enough to integrate into MESA" — quantitative gates.** Define, e.g.: per-step Ye error ≪ (target final-Ye accuracy)/(N_burn_steps). With Farmer 2016's ≈10% convergence target and ~10³–10⁴ burn steps in a pre-SN track, this implies a **per-step Ye error budget of ~10⁻⁵–10⁻⁴** (reasoned estimate from the convergence requirement). Energy error must be small enough not to perturb structure-solver convergence (a Phase-3 empirical gate). Require an OOD fallback-to-solver mechanism before any production run.

**THE SINGLE RISKIEST STEP: Phase 3 in-the-loop MESA stability.** Coupling a learned surrogate into the structure solver over 10³–10⁴ timesteps risks (a) error accumulation despite conservation (conservation bounds A and Z drift but *not* per-isotope or energy error), and (b) feedback instability where small e_nuc errors perturb the structure solve and push the burning zone OOD, compounding. This is also the step most exposed to being scooped, since the NuGNN group has already demonstrated in-solver substitution (for XRBs) and could pivot to CCSN progenitors. *Mitigation:* (i) the **Grichener collaboration is the moat** — they own bbq, the MESA split-burning machinery, and the exact CCSN-progenitor science case; co-authorship secures priority and de-risks the integration; (ii) a **hard fallback-to-solver gate** with OOD detection guarantees a publishable hybrid result even if pure-emulator runs are unstable; (iii) **front-load conservation and the offline benchmark (Phases 0–1)** so a strong Paper 1 is banked long before the risky integration.

## Caveats

- **Hardware/speedup comparisons are treacherous.** NuGNN's headline used a GPU (RTX 5090) for the surrogate vs. a CPU (Xeon Platinum 8360Y) for PRISM, with no clean factor stated; NuGNN is actually the *slowest* of its three NN surrogates (1.9 ms vs. FNN's 0.1 ms in TensorRT). Grichener's 20–34× is a cleaner, matched-context comparison. Do not repeat NuGNN's framing.
- **Conservation bounds A and Z, not everything.** Flux-form guarantees baryon/charge conservation to machine precision but does *not* bound per-isotope, energy, or Ye-evolution error — those still require the rollout/energy validation gates.
- **Size transfer is a hypothesis, not a result.** The GNS/MeshGraphNets precedent is for particle/mesh count, not for chemically-new neutron-rich isotopes; treat mesa_80→151 transfer as requiring fine-tuning until proven otherwise.
- **Compute estimates are extrapolations.** The 0.1–0.5 CPU-hr/sample figure is derived from Grichener's own "few×10⁵ CPU-hr per ~10⁶ samples"; GPU training-time and per-step error-budget figures are reasoned estimates, explicitly labeled, not cited benchmarks.
- **The 4 TB extended bbq dataset is "on request" only** — your variable-dt and extended-density plans load-bear on obtaining it; scope a smaller self-generated fallback set.
- **NuGNN (arXiv:2606.04491) was submitted 3 June 2026 and is preprint-stage** (likely not yet peer-reviewed); treat its specific numbers accordingly.
